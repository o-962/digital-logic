5 · Signed Number Representation

A computer can't store a "−" sign, so the leftmost bit is the sign: 0 = positive, 1 = negative. There are three systems, and they differ only in how they store negative numbers.

All three represent positive numbers the same way. They only differ for negative numbers.
2's complement is what real computers use: there's only one zero, it has one extra negative value, and addition just works (ignore the carry).

Reading a negative pattern (decoding)

2's complement: if the MSB is 1, take the 2's complement to get the magnitude, then add a minus sign. Or use the weight trick: the MSB has weight −2ⁿ⁻¹. For example 11001011 = −128 + 64 + 8 + 2 + 1 = −53.
1's complement: flip all bits to get the magnitude. Or give the MSB weight −(2ⁿ⁻¹ − 1).
Signed magnitude: MSB = sign, the remaining bits = magnitude.

"In 2's complement form" doesn't mean "take the 2's complement". If the number is positive, you just write it in binary with a 0 sign bit. Only negative numbers get complemented.

Sign extension

To widen a 2's complement (or 1's complement) number, copy the sign bit to the left. −3: 1101 (4 bits) → 1111 1101 (8 bits). For signed magnitude, insert zeros after the sign bit instead: 1011 → 1000 0011.

Signed addition in 2's complement + overflow

Add all bits including the sign bit and discard the carry out of the MSB. Overflow happens when two numbers with the same sign give a result with the opposite sign. Equivalently, the carry into the sign bit ≠ the carry out of it. Two numbers of opposite signs can never overflow.

Signed 10's complement (Book 1.19, 1.21)

Same idea in decimal: the sign digit is 0 for positive and 9 for negative. Negative numbers are stored as the 10's complement. Add, then discard the end carry.
